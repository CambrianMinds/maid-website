# OP-ED PITCHES
## Justin Bogner — Autonomy, Capacity, and the Right to Exit
## Two versions: General press + Medical/Bioethics press

---

## PITCH A — GENERAL PRESS
### Target: The Atlantic, The Guardian, New York Magazine, The Walrus (Canada)

**Subject line:** Op-ed pitch: The Case for Death Rights From Someone Who No Longer Wants to Die

---

Dear [Editor],

I'm pitching a 1,200-word op-ed on Medical Aid in Dying that I think would sit unusually well in [publication].

The hook: I survived a serious suicide attempt at eighteen. For nearly twenty years afterward, I lived under the low-grade presence of suicidal ideation — not always acute, always there. Eight months ago, it stopped. I am, by any measure, well.

I am also one of the most credible voices in the country for expanding the right to die.

The piece argues that the operative criterion for Medical Aid in Dying eligibility should be decisional capacity — not terminal illness, not documented suffering, not treatment compliance. And it makes that argument from the specific vantage point of someone who has been inside the coercive psychiatric system, emerged from it, and can now assess it without the distortion of active crisis.

The disability rights critique of expanded MAiD — that the state is offering death instead of adequate support — is taken seriously and engaged directly. My answer to it is what I call the forcing function: that an enforceable exit right, combined with mandatory documentation of socioeconomic drivers, creates a political mechanism for holding the state accountable for the conditions that produce these choices. The Protectionist position — lock the exit — has governed most jurisdictions for decades. In those same decades, housing supports have eroded and disability services have been cut. Closing the exit has not produced the dignified conditions its defenders claim it would compel.

The piece is grounded in current data. Health Canada's 2024 report documents 16,499 MAiD provisions, 5.1% of all Canadian deaths, with 61.5% of non-terminal recipients reporting disability and documented socioeconomic drivers appearing with increasing frequency. This is not hypothetical.

The personal frame is not incidental. The argument is stronger, not weaker, from a position of stability. Crisis produces claims that can be dismissed as symptoms. Stability produces claims that have to be answered.

I have a complete policy framework document available, and the piece can run at any length from 900 to 1,800 words depending on your needs.

Happy to provide a full draft on spec or to discuss scope.

Justin Bogner

---

**DRAFT LEDE (first 200 words for pitch packet):**

I was reading Health Canada's annual MAiD report when I did the thing I've been doing automatically for twenty years: ran a quick internal check. Suicidal ideation has been part of my neurological landscape since adolescence. I check for it the way some people check the weather.

The result was new. I didn't want to die.

Not performing recovery. Not white-knuckling it. Just: flat, clear, wouldn't. The hum that had been background noise for two decades had gone quiet.

I sat with it long enough to be sure I wasn't bullshitting myself. Then I felt something I can only describe as gratitude — not for some transformation, but for the specific relief of not fighting that particular war every day anymore.

And then, about thirty seconds later: *Even now. Even stable. My life is still mine to end if I choose to.*

That is not a crisis thought. That is a property rights claim. And it leads directly to the argument I want to make about why the legal framework for Medical Aid in Dying in most jurisdictions is philosophically incoherent — and why the case for reforming it is strongest when made by someone who has no personal stake in using it.

## PITCH B — MEDICAL / BIOETHICS PRESS
### Target: Journal of Medical Ethics, CMAJ (Canadian Medical Association Journal),
### Hastings Center Report, The Lancet (commentary), BMJ Opinion

**Subject line:** Commentary pitch: From Liability Theater to Therapeutic Alliance —
The Case for Capacity-Based MAiD Eligibility

---

Dear [Editor],

I am writing to propose a commentary for [journal/publication] on the philosophical and procedural case for replacing suffering-based MAiD eligibility criteria with a capacity-based framework.

The commentary would run approximately 1,500 words and engage directly with three bodies of work:

1. The informed refusal doctrine as established across Western medical jurisprudence, and the inconsistency of applying it everywhere except the domain of self-directed death
2. Health Canada's 2024 MAiD data, which documents escalating socioeconomic drivers in non-terminal Track 2 cases and raises legitimate questions about whether the state is adequately discharging its obligation to present alternatives
3. The disability rights critique (specifically DAWN Canada's position) as a serious objection requiring direct engagement rather than dismissal

**The central argument:** The clinical interview's role under a capacity-based framework is not to verify treatment compliance history but to assess comprehension of alternatives. The evaluating clinician asks not "have you tried an SSRI" but "what do you understand about SSRIs and what led you to decline them." An honest, documented informed refusal is more epistemically transparent — and clinically defensible — than coerced prior utilization producing performative compliance.

**The structural argument:** Where socioeconomic inadequacy is documented as a driver of MAiD preference, a mandatory reporting obligation to relevant government ministries creates a political mechanism for holding the state accountable. This is not a concession to the disability rights critique; it is the substantive answer to it. Categorical exclusion conceals systemic failure. Documentation exposes it.

**The author's position:** I write from an unusual vantage point: I survived a serious suicide attempt at eighteen, lived for nearly two decades with chronic suicidal ideation, and now, from a position of genuine stability, am developing this framework. I am not writing as a patient in crisis advocating for personal access. I am writing as someone who has been inside the coercive psychiatric system — safety contracts, pressured hospitalization, discharge with inadequate follow-up — and can evaluate it from outside.

The commentary is grounded in:
- Carter v. Canada (2015 SCC 5)
- Health Canada Sixth Annual Report (2024 data)
- Beauchamp & Childress, Principles of Biomedical Ethics (8th ed.)
- DAWN Canada's disability rights analysis (2023)
- Donnelly & Campbell, "Decisional Capacity and the Limits of Paternalism," Journal of Medical Ethics (2024)

A complete policy framework document is available for editorial review. I can provide a full draft on a timeline that suits your editorial schedule.

Justin Bogner

---

**DRAFT LEDE (bioethics version):**

Psychiatric response to suicidal ideation functions, in most institutional contexts, as liability management rather than clinical care. The operational priority is avoiding documented institutional responsibility for a death during treatment, not resolving the conditions that produced the crisis. The result is a cycle that is coercive in substance if not in name: pressured hospitalization, safety contracts of no demonstrated clinical value, premature discharge into unchanged material conditions, and repeat presentation.

This is not a treatment model. It is an administrative loop designed to defer rather than resolve.

A capacity-based framework for Medical Aid in Dying does not create new deaths. It creates a different relationship between clinician and patient — one in which the clinician, lacking the coercive power of custodial retention, is structurally required to become an ally in making the patient's life worth continuing, rather than a jailer mandating its continuation. This shift — from custodial to restorative — is not incidental to the framework. It is the framework's most consequential clinical implication.

## PUBLICATION NOTES

**For The Atlantic:** Emphasize the personal narrative frame and the counter-intuitive
stability angle. The Atlantic runs long personal-intellectual hybrids well.
Suggested word count: 1,400–1,800.

**For The Guardian:** Lead with the international comparators and Canada data.
The Guardian's readership is familiar with the MAiD debate from UK/Commonwealth angle.
Suggested word count: 1,000–1,200.

**For CMAJ:** Clinical framing first. The informed refusal doctrine argument is the hook.
The personal disclosure is secondary but important for establishing that this is not
purely theoretical. Suggested word count: 1,200–1,500.

**For Hastings Center Report:** This can run longer and more formally. Full engagement
with the philosophical literature. The disability rights section should be expanded.
Suggested word count: 2,000–2,500 (commentary format).

**For BMJ Opinion:** Short, sharp, deliberately provocative. Lead with the liability
theater critique. Suggested word count: 800–1,000.

## FOLLOW-UP STRATEGY

Send pitch. Wait 10 business days. Follow up once, briefly.
If no response: move to next target on the list. Do not chase.
The piece that gets accepted is the one that lands at the right editor
on the right week. The argument doesn't change; the timing is luck.

Simultaneous submission: most op-ed desks accept simultaneous pitches
(not simultaneous full submissions). Send pitches broadly.
Once a piece is accepted, withdraw immediately from all other outlets.
