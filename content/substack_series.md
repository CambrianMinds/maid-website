## POST 1 — THE ARRIVAL

**Title:** I Stopped Wanting to Die. I'm Still Fighting for the Right to.

**Purpose:** Establish your credibility, your voice, and the central paradox.
This is the hook. It must be linked everywhere.

**Epigraph:**
> "The choice to stay only means something when leaving is actually on the table."

---

I was doom-scrolling Canada's MAiD expansion data when I noticed something. I ran a quick internal check — the kind you learn to run automatically after years of carrying something heavy — and the result was new.

I didn't want to die.

Not in the performative "I've grown so much" way. Not the white-knuckle "I'm managing." Just: genuinely, flatly, wouldn't pull the trigger on it. The hum that had been background noise for twenty years had stopped.

I sat with it, suspicious I was bullshitting myself.

Then I laughed. Then I felt something I'd describe as gratitude — not for some revelation, but for the specific relief of not having to fight that particular war every day anymore.

And about thirty seconds after that: *Even now. Even happy. My life is still mine to torch if I feel like it.*

That's not a crisis thought. That's a property rights claim. And it turns out I have a lot to say about it.

---

**The short version of how we got here:**

At eighteen, I made a serious attempt. Not a cry for help. I knew the pharmacology. I did the math. I ate what I thought was a sufficient quantity of enough things and went to sleep expecting not to wake up.

I woke up. Three times, apparently, before I stayed. The medical team revived me without consent, which is their job and arguably correct, given that I was eighteen and three months of living and one bad night had shaped that decision.

What followed was the standard script: psychiatric hold, guilt ceremony, safety contract sworn on my mother's life, discharge with a fresh prescription for the same medication that almost worked. No follow-up plan. No housing support. No material change in the conditions that had produced the crisis. The system had successfully preserved my biology and done essentially nothing else.

I kept the promise I'd made. Not because I decided it was wrong to want out — I never concluded that. But because I loved the people who had asked me to make it, and that love was, for a long time, the only load-bearing wall.

That was eighteen years ago.

Now the wall is gone because it doesn't need to be a wall anymore. The house is standing on its own.

---

**Here's what I want you to hold while we do this together:**

The fact that I'm okay now does not mean my argument was wrong then. And it does not mean the argument is wrong now for the people who are not okay — the people the system has failed materially, the people for whom the conditions have not become tolerable, the people who are being held by legal coercion in lives they did not choose and cannot exit.

I'm making this case from stability. That's not a disqualifier. That's the whole point.

If I can look at this clearly — without crisis, without despair, without the particular gravity of active suicidal ideation shaping every premise — and still conclude that the right to exit is foundational, then the argument is not a symptom. It's a position.

---

**What's coming:**

This is the first in a series. By the end, I'll have laid out a complete alternative framework for non-terminal Medical Aid in Dying based on decisional capacity rather than suffering legibility. I'll have engaged the disability rights objections directly — because they are serious and they deserve direct engagement, not hand-waving. I'll have made the case that an enforceable right to exit is actually the most powerful structural argument for investing in dignified life that currently exists in policy discourse.

But we start here, because everything else is dishonest without it.

I tried to die. I survived. I spent nearly two decades trying to make peace with an existence I hadn't chosen to continue. The peace arrived not because someone forced me to stay, but because the conditions eventually became livable.

That is the argument.

The choice to stay only means something when leaving is actually on the table.

## POST 2 — THE WRONG CRITERION

**Title:** The Problem With "Unbearable": Why the Law Gets Suffering Wrong

**Purpose:** Introduce the philosophical core. Accessible but rigorous.
Audience: people who were moved by Post 1 and want the argument.

---

Here is the criterion most jurisdictions use to decide whether you are allowed to die:

**Unbearable suffering.**

Set aside for a moment what you think about whether that criterion is too permissive or not permissive enough. Just look at what it is. You must demonstrate, to the satisfaction of clinicians and gatekeepers, that your experience of your own life is sufficiently terrible to warrant recognition.

You must be legible. You must perform your pain in the correct register. You must be broken in the right way.

A calm person — someone who has thought carefully and arrived at a settled, philosophically reasoned preference for death — may be denied access on the grounds that they do not appear to be suffering enough. A person in visible, acute, articulable distress may be granted access more readily. The criterion rewards demonstration and punishes composure.

This is not a medical standard. It is a theater requirement.

---

**What's actually wrong with it:**

The suffering criterion conflates *sympathy* with *rights*. It says: we will recognize your autonomy when we feel sorry enough for you. It places the threshold of your self-determination inside the emotional response of the person reviewing your case.

But rights don't work that way. Or they shouldn't.

If you can refuse chemotherapy — and you can, anywhere in the Western world, without prior treatment, without justification, without anyone's approval — then you already possess the right to determine the conditions of your own body and existence. The question is only: why does that right evaporate the moment the determination is self-directed rather than treatment-directed?

The answer is not principled. It is aesthetic. A patient dying of untreated cancer by refusing chemotherapy is uncomfortable but comprehensible. A patient who simply decides that the aggregate of their life does not add up to continuation is uncomfortable in a way that implicates us — that asks questions we don't want answered.

So we built a criterion that screens for the former and excludes the latter. We call it compassion.

---

**What should replace it:**

One word: *capacity*.

Can you understand what you're deciding? Do you understand what death means — that it is permanent, irreversible, final? Do you understand what the alternatives are — not whether you've tried them, but whether you know they exist and what they realistically offer? Is your preference stable across time, not just a reaction to a bad Tuesday? Are you in a state — not a history, a *current state* — that permits genuine comprehension?

If yes: the rest is yours.

Not your family's. Not your clinician's. Not the state's. Yours.

---

**The double standard nobody talks about:**

You can refuse dialysis without ever having tried it. Your nephrologist is required to explain the treatment and its outcomes. If you understand and decline, that refusal is binding. It doesn't matter that dialysis might extend your life significantly. It doesn't matter that your family disagrees. It doesn't matter that your physician thinks you're making a mistake.

*Informed refusal. Full stop.*

Now tell me why that standard applies to dialysis and not to existence itself.

The answer, if you push hard enough on it, is always some version of: because we've decided that death-by-action is categorically different from death-by-inaction. That pulling a trigger is different from refusing to reload.

I don't think that distinction survives scrutiny. I think it is a distinction we maintain because the alternative — acknowledging that self-directed death can be a legitimate, autonomous, non-pathological choice — is socially and institutionally very uncomfortable.

Being uncomfortable is not a policy rationale.

## POST 3 — THE PROCESS

**Title:** The Safeguard That Isn't Paranoia: Why Good Process Changes Everything

**Purpose:** The procedural argument. Addresses the "but what about abuse?" concern.
Shows this framework is rigorous, not reckless.

---

The most common objection I hear when I describe a capacity-based framework for non-terminal MAiD is some version of: *But people will abuse it. People in crisis will use it. Vulnerable people will be pressured into it.*

These are real concerns. They deserve real answers. Not dismissal, not hand-waving — actual procedural architecture that addresses them.

Here's what that looks like.

---

**Stage 1: Can you actually understand what you're deciding?**

Not "are you in enough pain." Not "have you tried enough treatments." Just: do you understand what you're deciding?

That means: you know death is permanent and irreversible. You know what the alternatives are — not just that they exist, but what they realistically offer and what declining them means. You're not currently in a state — psychosis, acute crisis, severe cognitive impairment — that is materially distorting your comprehension. And your preference has been stable across time, not just a reaction to this particular week.

Note what this does *not* include: a requirement that you have tried any of the alternatives. You can decline dialysis without trial. You can decline chemotherapy without trial. You can decline psychiatric intervention without trial. The requirement is that you *understand* what you're declining. That is the informed refusal standard medicine already uses everywhere else.

---

**Stage 2: Can you understand why we're checking?**

This is where it gets interesting.

The process itself is diagnostic. A person in acute crisis will very rarely tolerate a 90-day bureaucratic procedure with multiple independent evaluations. The impulse to exit *right now* — which is what crisis looks like — is incompatible with the patience that structured assessment requires.

A person with a resolved, stable preference can wait 90 days. Not happily, maybe. But they can. The process itself distinguishes the two states more reliably than any single clinical interview.

There's a further point. If you genuinely have capacity — if you understand what you're deciding — you understand *why the safeguards exist*. You understand that they're protecting you from your own potential future crisis states, and protecting against external coercion, and distinguishing your case from the cases that shouldn't proceed. A person who has capacity but refuses to engage with the safeguards — who insists on bypassing the process — is demonstrating, in that refusal, that their relationship to this decision is not what capacity requires.

Process comprehension is a component of capacity. Failing Stage 2 while claiming Stage 1 is a contradiction.

---

**The coercion problem:**

It's real. Elderly people, disabled people, people who experience themselves as burdens — these populations are genuinely vulnerable to subtle or explicit pressure from family members, financial incentives, inadequate care systems.

The answer is not categorical exclusion of vulnerable people from eligibility. That is its own form of coercion — it overrides the expressed preference of everyone in these categories in order to protect against the victimization of some.

The answer is process:

- Confidential interview with an independent social worker or patient advocate who has no institutional stake in the outcome.
- Structured review of financial circumstances and dependency relationships.
- Mandatory offer of independent legal assistance.
- Absolute, unconditional right to revoke at any moment, with no adverse consequence.

These are not perfect protections. There are no perfect protections. But they are substantive protections — and they are more substantive than the current system, which relies on suffering legibility as a screen and coercive hospitalization as a backstop.

---

**On waiting periods:**

I've been asked: what's the magic number? 90 days? 6 months? A year?

The honest answer is: I don't know the magic number, and neither does anyone else. Belgium's non-terminal cases have a shorter floor. Canada's Track 2 cases have 90 days. The Netherlands is minimal.

Here's what I know: if the resolve is genuine and stable, time just becomes preparation and peace. The truly resolved don't flinch at calendars. The person who can't wait 90 days is telling you something important about the stability of the preference.

What I'd argue against is open-ended waiting — the "come back in six months and we'll see" non-answer that functions as administrative attrition. Set the floor. Hold it. Then let the adult proceed.

## POST 4 — THE HARD CONVERSATION

**Title:** The Disability Rights Critique Is Serious. Here's Why It Points the Wrong Way.

**Purpose:** Direct engagement with DAWN Canada / ADAPT / disability rights critics.
This post will generate controversy. That is the point. Do not soften.
This is the most important post in the series for building serious credibility.

---

I want to start by saying something directly: the disability rights organizations that oppose expanded MAiD eligibility are not wrong about the problem.

DAWN Canada, ADAPT, and the researchers who have documented the socioeconomic drivers of MAiD uptake in Canada have identified a real and serious failure. Health Canada's own 2024 data shows that Track 2 recipients — the non-terminal cases — are modestly more likely to reside in lower-income neighborhoods, and that disability and social isolation are prominent documented drivers of "intolerable suffering" claims. The Ontario Chief Coroner's office has documented cases where inadequate housing and income insufficiency appear as primary conditions motivating MAiD requests.

These are not anomalies. They are signals. And the disability rights organizations are right to treat them as signals of systemic failure.

Where they're wrong is in the conclusion.

---

**The Protectionist conclusion:**

Close the exit. Restrict eligibility. If the state is offering death as a substitute for adequate support, the answer is to remove the offer.

This sounds like protection. It functions as abandonment with extra steps.

The person who lacks housing and care, and who finds their life intolerable as a result, is not made better off by being denied the right to act on that assessment. They are made worse off in a way that is now also legally compelled. They remain in the conditions that produced the crisis, with the added dimension that the state has decided it knows better than they do whether those conditions are sufficient to justify exit.

The Protectionist position has governed most jurisdictions for decades. In those same decades: housing supports have eroded, disability services have been chronically underfunded, psychiatric wait times have grown. There is no evidence — none — that coercing existence produces political pressure for better lives. The people trapped in inadequate conditions do not become a political constituency by virtue of being trapped. They become invisible.

---

**The forcing function:**

Here is the alternative:

Under this framework, evaluators are required to document every alternative they present — housing, income support, disability services, community programs — and whether the applicant accepted or declined. Where an applicant identifies inadequate access to these supports as a condition of their preference, the administering authority is required to transmit a formal report to the relevant government ministry. Not a private administrative note. A documented, aggregable, politically legible record: *this person chose death because the state did not provide adequate housing.*

That record is a political weapon of the first order.

The disability rights organizations want the state to improve conditions before unlocking the exit. I understand the logic. But we've been waiting for the state to improve conditions with the exit locked. We know what happens. Nothing. Or worse than nothing: slow erosion of the little that exists.

Unlock the exit, and document every choice made in the shadow of state failure. Make those deaths part of the public record. Create a political cost for the failure that is specific, attributed, and undeniable.

That is the structural incentive the Protectionist position cannot create. You cannot shame a state into caring about invisible suffering. You can potentially shame it into caring about documented, state-recorded deaths attributed to its own negligence.

---

**The thing I'll say plainly:**

If the choice is between:
(a) giving people access to dignity — either adequate support or a clean exit — and
(b) denying them both while calling the denial compassion,

then (b) is not a disability rights position. It is a management position dressed as one.

Disabled people are not owed continued existence in inadequate conditions. They are owed the same right to self-determination that everyone else has. The framework for ensuring they exercise that right without coercion is process — robust, documented, independently monitored process — not categorical exclusion.

## POST 5 — THE FORCING FUNCTION

**Title:** Unlock the Door, and the Room Must Improve or Empty: The Right to Exit as Political Tool

**Purpose:** The systemic argument. This is the one that should get shared by people
who are not already converted. It's the most counter-intuitive claim and the most important.

---

I want to make an argument that will sound, on first read, like it's about death. It's actually about politics.

Here it is:

**The right to exit — a legally enforceable right to choose death on your own terms — is the most powerful structural argument for investing in dignified life that currently exists in policy discourse.**

Not because death is good. Because documentation is powerful. Because the state responds to political costs, and right now the cost of failing people is essentially invisible.

---

**The current dynamic:**

A person lives in inadequate housing. Their disability is undertreated. Their isolation is chronic. They find their life intolerable. They tell their doctor. Their doctor — operating in a system designed to prevent liability, not resolve conditions — initiates a coercive hospitalization cycle. Biology is preserved. The conditions remain unchanged. The person cycles back through the same system, or stops talking to the system, or eventually dies in circumstances that are attributed to their condition rather than to the failure of support.

This is not tragedy. It is policy outcome. And it is invisible because there is no mechanism for making it legible.

---

**What changes with an enforceable exit right:**

Under this framework, every MAiD application must document the alternatives presented and whether the applicant could access them. Where an applicant identifies inadequate housing, income, or care as a driver of their preference, the administering authority is required to formally report this to the relevant ministry.

Imagine that data, aggregated. Imagine the political document it creates: *In 2027, 94 Canadians received MAiD in circumstances where documented inadequacy of housing, disability support, or income were identified as primary contributing conditions.* 

That number has a face. Those faces have a story. That story has a ministry. That ministry has a minister. That minister has an election to survive.

The locked exit produces no such document. The person who cannot access MAiD and lives on in inadequate conditions generates no political record. Their suffering is private and administrative. Their continued existence is counted as a policy success.

---

**The Survivor's Dividend:**

If this framework is adopted and functioning well, the expected outcomes are:
1. Some people will choose to exit. Their deaths, where attributable to systemic failure, will be documented and politically attributed.
2. That political record creates structural pressure for investment in support systems.
3. More people will have access to adequate housing, disability support, and community connection — and will therefore not choose to exit.
4. The people who choose to remain do so in conditions that have been materially improved by the political pressure created by those who did not.

This is what I mean by the Survivor's Dividend. It is not a guarantee. It is a structural incentive that currently does not exist. The Protectionist Model creates no such incentive, because it makes the failure invisible by making the exit impossible.

Unlock the door. Make the room compete for occupancy.

## POST 6 — THE ARCHITECTURE

**Title:** How to Do This Without Being Monsters: The Procedural Case

**Purpose:** Consolidation post. Brings together the process argument with the
philosophical argument. For the audience that wants specifics.
Shorter than the others. Punchy. List-heavy by design here — this one is
meant to be referenced and shared as a resource.

---

People ask me: *Okay, but how do you build this without it becoming a killing machine for inconvenient people?*

Fair question. Here's the architecture.

---

**The eligibility criterion:**

Decisional capacity. That's it. Not terminal diagnosis. Not demonstrated suffering. Not treatment compliance history.

Decisional capacity means: you understand what you're deciding. You understand what death is. You know the alternatives exist and what they realistically offer. You're not in a state that materially distorts your comprehension. Your preference has been stable across time.

---

**The process:**

*90-day minimum assessment period for non-terminal cases.* Not arbitrary — diagnostic. The process itself distinguishes resolved preference from crisis.

*Multiple independent evaluations.* Not the same clinician twice. Not the treating team with a vested interest in the outcome. Independent.

*Mandatory alternatives presentation.* Every realistic option — housing, income support, disability services, psychiatric intervention, peer support, palliative care — must be presented with sufficient specificity for genuine comprehension. Not a pamphlet. A documented conversation. Referrals offered. Responses recorded.

*Independent coercion screening.* A social worker with no institutional stake in the outcome. A structured review of financial and dependency circumstances. A mandatory offer of legal assistance.

*Absolute revocation right.* Until the final moment. No explanation required. No adverse consequence. No locked-in death date.

*Socioeconomic documentation requirement.* Where inadequate support is identified as a driver, the state documents it formally and reports it to the relevant ministry. This is not optional. This is the forcing function.

---

**What this filters out:**

Impulse cases. People in acute crisis rarely tolerate 90 days of structured bureaucracy. They want out now. The process is incompatible with that urgency in a way that a single clinical interview is not.

Coerced cases. Independent screening, financial review, and revocation rights create multiple exit ramps for people whose "choice" is actually pressure.

Crisis states. The evaluation explicitly distinguishes stable preference from current acute crisis. A history of depression does not disqualify. Active psychosis at the time of evaluation does.

---

**What this does not filter out:**

People with psychiatric histories who are currently stable and have a settled, informed preference.

People whose primary driver is socioeconomic inadequacy — because filtering them out would be filtering out the state's own failure, and we need that failure documented, not hidden.

People who have never tried the alternatives — because the standard is informed refusal, not prior utilization. Same as everywhere else in medicine.

## POST 7 — THE CLOSE

**Title:** I Don't Want to Die. I Want the Right to. And So Should You.

**Purpose:** The close. Call to action. What the reader can actually do.
This is the post that asks something of people.

---

I've spent six weeks making an argument. Let me be very direct about what I'm actually asking for.

---

I don't want to die. I want to say that without performance, because it matters to the argument. I'm stable, I'm working, I'm choosing this daily — the farm, the writing, the people I love, the work that feels like mine. I am a genuine volunteer for this particular existence.

Which is why the right to leave is not abstract to me.

The choice to stay only carries moral weight if leaving was actually available. Otherwise it's not a choice; it's a default. I'm not interested in my continued existence being a default. I want it to be a decision.

---

**What I'm asking you to do:**

If you've read this series and you think the argument holds, here are the specific actions that create pressure:

**1. Read and share the policy document.**
The full framework — the philosophical foundation, the procedural architecture, the international comparators, the disability rights engagement, the model statutory language — is available at [link]. Share it. Send it to people who make decisions.

**2. Name your legislators and contact them.**
If you're in a jurisdiction where MAiD legislation is live or pending — Canada, any US state currently considering expansion, any European jurisdiction with active debates — find the specific legislators on the relevant committees and write to them. Not form letters. Personal letters that reference the framework.

**3. Engage the disability rights organizations directly.**
DAWN Canada, ADAPT, Disability Rights Advocates. Don't dismiss them. Engage them. The forcing function argument is the answer to their concern — but it has to be a conversation, not a rebuttal from distance.

**4. If you work in clinical medicine or psychiatry:** Talk about this with your colleagues. The clinical culture of coercive retention is not policy; it is habit. Habits change in conversations.

**5. If you've been through this yourself** — as a patient, as a family member, as a clinician who has participated in something you did not believe in — you have a story that matters. Tell it. In your own register. In your own voice. Not my voice, which is one voice with one history.

---

**The thing I want to leave you with:**

This is not a death-positive campaign. It is a sovereignty campaign.

I am not arguing that death is better than life. I am arguing that the choice belongs to the person whose life it is — and that a choice is only real when the alternative is actually available.

A society that lets people stay only because it has locked the exit is not a society that values life. It is a society that has confused coercion with care.

Unlock the door. Make the room worth staying in. Document every time it isn't.

That's the whole argument.

I'm still here. I'm glad I am. And I will keep making this case for everyone who isn't.
